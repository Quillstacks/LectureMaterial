\section{The Why}\index{remote access}\index{attack surface}

\newthought{The engine answers on the home network} and trusts anyone who can reach it. That is
fine for a browser in the kitchen and useless for the rest of life: the laptop on a
train, the phone in another country, the coding agent in your terminal at work. This
chapter makes the box reachable from anywhere without exposing it to everyone, and
turns it into something your other tools can authenticate against.

\marginnote{\newthought{Two different jobs.} Reaching the box yourself from your own
devices is one problem; letting the family open a link without installing anything is
another; letting a program log in with a key is a third. They want three different
answers, not one.}

\newthought{The danger to respect} is that Ollama has no password and was built to
listen only to its own machine. The lazy fix, binding it to the network directly,
hands your GPU to the internet\index{port forwarding}. So nothing is exposed raw. Instead a private network
carries you to the box, a tunnel carries the family, and Open WebUI issues keys for
programs.

\newthought{By the end of this chapter} you will have:
\begin{itemize}
    \item reached Open WebUI from your phone and laptop anywhere, over a private mesh;
    \item opened a browser link for the family that needs no VPN;
    \item issued an API key and driven the engine from a terminal coding agent off-site.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reach and Auth}\index{mesh VPN}\index{tunnel}\index{authentication}

\newthought{Tailscale} builds a private encrypted mesh between your own devices.
Install it on the rig and on your laptop and phone, and they reach each other by a
stable name as if on one LAN, through firewalls and carrier NAT\index{NAT traversal}, with no port
forwarded and nothing published. The tailnet itself is the authentication: only
your enrolled devices\index{tailnet} can connect, so the box is reachable everywhere by you and
invisible to everyone else\cite{tailscaleSelfHostAI}.

\marginnote{\newthought{Mesh versus tunnel.} A mesh joins devices you own into one
private network. A tunnel publishes one service to the public web behind a login.
The first is for you on the move; the second is for people who will not install a
VPN. Most homelabs run both.}

\newthought{Cloudflare Tunnel}\index{Cloudflare!Tunnel} solves the other case: a relative who just wants a
link. A small daemon on the rig dials out to Cloudflare, which serves your chat at a
real hostname with automatic TLS\index{TLS termination}, and Cloudflare Access\index{Cloudflare!Access} puts a login in
front\cite{cloudflareTunnel}. The
sovereignty cost is real: Cloudflare terminates the TLS and sees the metadata of
who connects when, though not what runs on your hardware. Use it for the family
front door, not for your private traffic.

\newthought{Open WebUI is the key issuer.}\index{Open WebUI!API keys} Programs do not log in through a browser;
they send a key. Open WebUI already holds the accounts, and it exposes an
OpenAI-compatible endpoint\index{OpenAI-compatible API} at \texttt{/api} where each user can mint API keys. A
terminal coding agent\index{coding agent} or an editor points at that endpoint with a bearer key\index{bearer token} and
talks to the same models, authenticated and logged per user, never touching Ollama
directly\cite{openwebuiAPI}.

\section{Use It Anywhere}\index{tailscale serve}\index{Tailscale}

\marginnote{\newthought{Install once per device.} Tailscale goes on the rig and on
every device you want to reach it from. The phone and laptop apps are a login, not a
config file.}

\newthought{Join the rig to your mesh}, then reach it by name:

\begin{verbatim}
$ curl -fsSL https://tailscale.com/install.sh | sh
$ sudo tailscale up                    # log in once in the browser
$ tailscale serve --bg 3000            # Open WebUI over HTTPS on the tailnet
\end{verbatim}

\newthought{Open it from anywhere.} With Tailscale running on your phone, browse to
the rig's tailnet name; Open WebUI loads from your own hardware over an encrypted
link, on cellular, with nothing exposed to the public internet.

\marginnote{\newthought{The family door}, optionally. A Cloudflare tunnel publishes
the same chat at a public hostname with a login: \texttt{cloudflared tunnel create
rig}, route a hostname to \texttt{localhost:3000}, \texttt{cloudflared tunnel run},
then add a Cloudflare Access policy limiting it to your family's emails.}

\newthought{Mint a key} in Open WebUI under Settings, Account, then drive the engine
from a program. Any OpenAI-compatible client works; point it at the \texttt{/api}
endpoint with the key:

\begin{verbatim}
$ export OPENAI_API_BASE=https://rig.your-tailnet.ts.net/api
$ export OPENAI_API_KEY=sk-...         # from Open WebUI > Settings > Account
$ curl $OPENAI_API_BASE/chat/completions \
    -H "Authorization: Bearer $OPENAI_API_KEY" \
    -d '{"model":"qwen3:14b","messages":[{"role":"user","content":"hi"}]}'
\end{verbatim}

\marginnote{\newthought{Coding agents.}\index{coding agent} Aider, OpenCode, and Cline all take an
OpenAI base URL and key, so the two variables above are the whole setup. Even Claude
Code can be pointed home with \texttt{ANTHROPIC\_BASE\_URL} set to the same
\texttt{/api} and \texttt{ANTHROPIC\_AUTH\_TOKEN} set to your key.}

\newthought{What you now own} travels with you. From a laptop on a foreign network,
your terminal coding agent reads the two variables, sends a task to the rig at home,
and edits your code on a model nobody meters.
